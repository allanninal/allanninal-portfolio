Finished in January. Out in September.

A record is finished in January and comes out in September, and seven of those nine months are a queue nobody in the chain controls. This page publishes the calendar — what is work, what is waiting, what can be moved and what it costs — because an artist who knows that in month one is a different artist from one who finds out in month five.

Belle Plaine — “Kanopolis” · Masters delivered 14 January 2026

254 days. 144 of them are a queue.

That is 8.3 months from a finished master to a record in a shop, of which 4.7 months is waiting and 5.1 months is work — and the single longest bar on the chart, pressing queue at 118 days, is longer than every piece of work on it added together. Solid bars are work. Outlined bars are waiting. None of the waiting is anybody in this chain being slow — the plant is not slow, the plant is full, which is a different problem and not one an artist can fix by asking twice. The useful thing about seeing it laid out is that it is knowable in month one. Every argument a label has with an artist about a release date is an argument about a chart nobody drew.

Work Waiting Day 0 is masters delivered · Released 25 September 2026

  1. MasteringUs 11 days from day 0 Two attended passes and a week between them, which is a week of listening rather than a week of waiting. The only stage on this list where more money buys more quality instead of a better place in a line.
  2. Plant slot bookedUs 1 day from day 4 Booked on day four, eleven days before mastering is even signed off. A slot booked in March is a September record and a slot booked in June is next year, so the booking has to come first. It feels reckless and it is the opposite.
  3. Lacquer cut and platingPlant 17 days from day 11 Real work by real people, and the last point at which the audio can still change. After this the record is a physical object being manufactured.
  4. Test pressing in the postNobody 12 days from day 28 Two discs travelling from the plant to a kitchen table in Kansas. Twelve days of the release calendar spent in a padded envelope, and there is no version of this that is faster.
  5. Test pressing approvalArtist 6 days from day 40 The artist listens on two systems and says yes or no. Six days because people have jobs. A no here costs eleven weeks and it is still the right answer if the answer is no.
  6. Pressing queuePlant 118 days from day 46 A hundred and eighteen days in which nothing whatsoever happens to your record. This single bar is longer than every piece of work on this page added together, and it is the reason the whole calendar looks the way it does.
  7. Sleeves printedPrinter 21 days from day 118 Runs inside the pressing window, which is the one piece of good news on this list. Artwork is due here, not at the end, and a late sleeve is the most avoidable delay in the business.
  8. Long-lead press embargoUs 56 days from day 198 Monthlies and long-lead press need the record eight weeks before it exists in shops. The version they get is a stream, not a record, which is its own small indignity.
  9. Distribution cutoffDistributor 1 day from day 208 The hardest date on the page. Miss it by one day and the release moves by a month, because the calendar is a grid and there is no space between the lines.
  10. Radio servicingUs 42 days from day 212 Six weeks out. Overlaps the press window on purpose — a station that hears about a record only from a press release has already decided.
  11. Records arrive with usFreight 14 days from day 164 Palletised, from the plant to a storage unit off 23rd. Two weeks and one phone call about a loading dock.
  12. Release dayEveryone 1 day from day 254 A Friday, always, because the charts week starts on one. Two hundred and fifty-four days after the masters were finished.

Eight stages, and two of them are walls

What can actually be moved.

Most of a release calendar is negotiable and the two pieces that are not happen to be the two everybody assumes are. Every row says what moving it costs and what it costs you.

Two dates decide the year.

The plant booking, day 4
Made before mastering is signed off and long before anyone has heard a test pressing. A slot booked in March presses in July and ships in September; the same booking made in June is a record that comes out next year. Every label that looks disorganised about release dates is a label that booked its slot late once.
The distributor’s cutoff, day 208
Metadata, artwork, audio and barcodes, all delivered six to eight weeks before the release date, on a date the distributor sets and does not move. Missing it does not delay the record by a week. It delays it by a month, because the release calendar is a grid.

Three shapes of deal.

Licence, three years
We pay for the pressing and the campaign, we license the recording for three years, and it reverts to the artist automatically with no letter to write. Splits are 60/40 to the artist after costs. This is what most records here are.
Pressing and distribution only
The artist keeps everything, we take 20% of trade sales and put the record through our distributor. No press, no radio, no advance. For an artist who has an audience and needs a pressing line rather than a label.
One record, no option
A single release, no right to the next one, no option clause. Every artist here started on this and two of them are still on it. An option clause on a first record is a label asking for a favour it has not earned.

Common questions

Mostly about the queue.

Why does it take nine months?

About seven weeks of it is work. The rest is a pressing plant queue of roughly four months, twelve days of test pressings in the post, and eight weeks of press embargo that has to sit in front of the release date rather than behind it. Nobody in the chain is being slow. The plant is full, which is a different problem and not one an artist can fix by asking twice.

Can we just release it digitally while we wait?

You can and it usually costs you the vinyl campaign. Press treats a record as new once, radio the same, and a digital release six months early spends that once. The exception is a single, which is what singles are for.

Do you take the masters?

No. The standard deal here is a three-year licence and the recording reverts automatically at the end of it with nothing to sign and no letter to write. Two of the four artists on this roster are on a one-record deal with no option clause, which is the shape I would want if I were the artist.

What happens if we reject the test pressing?

You lose about eleven weeks and you should still do it if it needs doing. One record on this roster went back over a warp on side B, came out four months late, and is the one people actually know. A bad pressing is permanent; a delay is not.

Will you listen to our demo?

Yes, on a Monday, all of it if the recording lets me hear the song. Twelve hundred and forty arrived in 2025 and sixty-one got a real reply with a reason. Two were signed, which is not a filter working well — it is how many records a four-artist label can physically put out.

Do we need a manager or a lawyer to talk to you?

No. The contract is four pages, the splits are on this page, and I will happily wait while somebody reads it. Any label that needs you to have representation before it will explain its own deal is telling you about the deal.
