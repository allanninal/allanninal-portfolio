Why the card lies

Free costs nothing to stop

Two inches, under shop lights Prevents the expensive mistake

A paint chip is about two inches square, printed rather than painted, viewed under retail lighting and surrounded by fifty other colours. Every one of those distorts it. The card is a shortlisting tool and nothing more; it cannot tell you what a wall will look like.

  • ✓ Colours read lighter on a card than on a wall
  • ✓ Surrounding colours on the card shift what you see
  • ✓ Retail lighting is nothing like domestic lighting
  • ✓ Printed ink is not the same pigment as the paint
  • ✓ Use cards to narrow to five, never to choose

Sample properly

The only test that works

A repaint costs 5 to 10 times more The cheapest part of the job

Large samples, painted on the actual walls, on more than one elevation, left up long enough to see in morning light, afternoon light and under your own lamps at night. This is the entire method, and it is the step people skip.

  • ✓ At least A3 sized, ideally larger
  • ✓ Two coats, so you see the real colour not the primer through it
  • ✓ On two different walls — one facing the window, one facing away
  • ✓ Never on white card; the white edge shifts your perception
  • ✓ Left up across two full days before deciding
  • ✓ Looked at at the times of day you actually use the room

Sheen

Sheen changes a colour more than most people expect.

Sheen is how much light the surface bounces back, and it changes both the apparent colour and how the wall wears. It is chosen per surface, not per room: the same colour usually wants a different sheen on a wall, a ceiling and a skirting board.

Flat / matt

Non-member Ceilings, low-traffic walls
Plan member Reads deepest

Hides an uneven wall better than anything else, and marks the most easily. The right answer for a ceiling almost always.

Matt emulsion

Non-member Living rooms, bedrooms
Plan member Slight softness

The default for walls. Modern washable matts have largely solved the old problem of not being able to clean them.

Eggshell

Non-member Hallways, kitchens, trim
Plan member Lifts the colour

Enough sheen to wipe, little enough not to spotlight a bad wall. The usual choice for woodwork.

Satin

Non-member Bathrooms, doors
Plan member Noticeably brighter

Handles moisture and handling. On a large wall it starts showing every imperfection in the plaster.

Gloss

Non-member Period trim, railings
Plan member Brightest, hardest

Traditional on Victorian woodwork and unforgiving of preparation. Every sanding mark will be visible.

Common Mistakes

The five that cause almost every repaint we get called about.

None of these are about taste. They are about method, and every one of them is avoidable in an afternoon.

Choosing from a chip

Two inches, printed, under shop lights, next to fifty other colours. It is a shortlisting tool. Every colour regret we are called about started here.

Sampling on one wall only

The wall facing the window and the wall facing away are effectively two different colours. Sampling one and painting both is how a room ends up half right.

Deciding in the middle of the day

Look at the sample in the morning, in the late afternoon, and at night under your own lamps. A colour that only works at noon is a colour you will see wrong most of the time.

Painting the ceiling the same white

The same white on the wall and the ceiling makes the ceiling look grubby, because it gets less light. Take the ceiling a step lighter, or a dedicated ceiling white.

Choosing the sheen last

Matt reads darker than eggshell in the same colour. If sheen is decided after the colour is signed off, the colour on the wall is not the one that was approved.

None of this is proprietary and none of it needs us. If you take this page and do it yourself with another painter, it will still work — which is rather the point of writing it down.
