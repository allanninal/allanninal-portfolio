The document itself

The pull letter

What a pull letter is

Also called a Letter of Responsibility — the agreement between the lender (a designer, a PR showroom, a boutique) and the stylist naming who is financially responsible for loss, theft or damage, and stating the return date. Nothing leaves a showroom without one.

Why it exists

A dress lent for a two-day shoot that isn't back and re-listed by its due-back date is lost revenue for the label that made it. A showroom's decision to lend to Noor again rests entirely on whether that date, and this document, were honoured last time.

Mood board to follow-up

How a pull works

Seven steps, always in this order — the same sequence behind every look on the rail above.

  1. Mood board A direction — silhouette, palette, references — shared with the photographer and, for advertising work, the brand before any garment is requested.
  2. Showroom request, during market Specific pieces requested from each PR showroom against the mood board, timed to that season’s market appointment window.
  3. Pull letter signed Before anything leaves the showroom, a Letter of Responsibility is signed naming who’s financially responsible, the shoot dates, and the return date — see below.
  4. Pickup Garments collected in person or via a bonded courier, checked against the pull letter’s itemised list piece by piece.
  5. Shoot Steamed or pressed on set immediately before camera — every look is checked for pulls, creases and fit right up to the first frame.
  6. Return by due date Every piece goes back to its showroom by the date on the pull letter, re-listed and back on the sales floor or sample rack the same week.
  7. Follow-up A tearsheet or usage confirmation sent back to the showroom once the piece runs — closing the loop that got the label credited in the first place.

Lanes & rates

The three lanes

Editorial
Regional glossies and brand editorial — the lowest day rate of the three lanes, paid in access, credit and tearsheets as much as cash. Where most of Noor’s calendar lives.
Advertising & campaign
Paid brand campaigns and catalogue work — the highest day rate, plus a separately negotiated usage or buyout fee for how the resulting images are used.
Private client
A small, clearly separate line: press days and red-carpet appearances for individual clients. Not a closet-audit or personal-shopping retail practice — no capsule-wardrobe packages offered.

Day rate, usage fee, kit fee — three separate numbers

A day rate pays for time on set. A usage or buyout fee is a separate, negotiated charge for how and how long a client may use the resulting images. A kit fee covers Noor's own working kit. Public data on stylist rates is thin — the figures below are stated as ranges from named sources, not a single invented number. See RESEARCH.md for sourcing.

FAQ

Questions

Do you shop as well as style?

Occasionally, for the private-client line — a press day or a red-carpet appearance sometimes needs a piece sourced and purchased rather than pulled. That’s a small, separate line item from editorial or advertising work, and it’s never a closet-audit or capsule-wardrobe retail service.

What happens if something’s damaged on set?

The pull letter is exactly the document that answers this: it names who’s financially responsible and states the piece’s replacement value before it ever leaves the showroom. Minor issues (a scuff, a loose thread) are flagged to the showroom immediately; anything beyond that follows the loss/damage clause on the signed letter.

On-figure or flat lay — how do you decide?

On-figure shows drape, movement and how a piece actually sits on a body — the default for anything with structure or tailoring. Flat lay is faster, cheaper and better suited to an accessories or capsule story where the garment itself, not the fit, is the point — two of the looks on the rail below are shot that way on purpose.
