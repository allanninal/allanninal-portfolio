Field guide

Eight things people call us about.

Each entry carries a threshold — the point where treatment is actually warranted. Three say monitor, one says leave it alone.

Swarms March–May

Subterranean termites

Treat now

How you know
Mud tubes on foundation walls, hollow-sounding trim, discarded wings on sills in spring.
Threshold
Any confirmed activity. This is the one pest where waiting genuinely costs you thousands.

Year-round, indoors

German cockroach

Treat now

How you know
Small, tan, two dark stripes behind the head. Seen in daylight means a large population.
Threshold
Any sighting indoors. They breed too fast for monitoring to be a strategy.

Spring through autumn

Odorous house ants

Monitor

How you know
A trail to one food source; crushed, they smell faintly of blue cheese.
Threshold
A steady indoor trail for more than a week. A single scouting line after rain usually stops on its own.

Moves indoors October–February

House mouse

Treat now

How you know
Droppings along wall runs, gnawed packaging, scratching in the ceiling after dark.
Threshold
Any indoor evidence. Exclusion first — sealing the entry beats trapping what already got in.

Peaks August–September

Yellowjackets

Treat now

How you know
Steady traffic to a single gap in siding or a hole in the lawn.
Threshold
A nest within about 20 feet of a door, a play area, or anyone with a sting allergy. Otherwise a nest in a far fence line can be left to die off in winter.

April–October

Mosquitoes

Monitor

How you know
Biting at dawn and dusk, worst within a few days of rain.
Threshold
Standing water is the treatment. We will walk the yard and empty it with you before we quote a barrier spray, because the spray without that is money burned.

Year-round

Common house spider

Leave it

How you know
Untidy cobwebs in corners, ceilings and window reveals.
Threshold
None. They eat the things you actually mind and they are not a structural or health risk. We will knock the webs down while we are there and not charge you for a treatment.

Year-round, humidity-driven

Silverfish

Monitor

How you know
Fast, wingless, tapered; usually in bathrooms, basements and boxes of old paper.
Threshold
Persistent numbers, which almost always means a humidity problem. Fix the damp and they leave.

Why the first answer is often “not yet”

Integrated pest management, in four steps.

Not a marketing position — it is the EPA's and the university extension services'. Broadcast spraying is explicitly the last resort, and gardens managed this way use 60–90% less synthetic pesticide for comparable results.

  1. 01

    Identify

    Half the calls we take are for something that is not what the caller thinks it is, and a third are for something that does not need treating at all. Nothing sensible happens before identification.

  2. 02

    Set a threshold

    One ant is not an infestation. A threshold is the point where damage or risk becomes unacceptable — and it is different for a wasp nest by the back door than for one in a far hedge.

  3. 03

    Prevent

    Exclusion, moisture, food storage, habitat. This is the least glamorous part and it is the part that actually holds.

  4. 04

    Control, least-risk first

    Traps, baits and targeted application before anything broadcast. Broadcast spraying is the last resort, not the opening move.

Common questions

Before you book.

Is quarterly spraying actually necessary?

For a lot of homes, no. A quarterly plan makes sense if you have recurring pressure — ants every spring, roaches from a shared wall, a wooded lot. If you called once about one problem, a one-time visit and some exclusion work is usually the better spend, and we will tell you that.

Do you use chemicals inside?

Rarely, and never as a first move. Most indoor work is baiting, trapping and sealing. When an interior application is genuinely warranted we tell you the product, where it goes, and how long to stay out.

Is the treatment safe for pets and children?

The products are applied at label rates and the label is the law. That said, "safe" depends entirely on following re-entry intervals, so we give you them in writing. If you have fish, reptiles or nursing pets, tell us before we start — those change what we use.

Why is your termite inspection free when others charge?

Because it is how we get the chance to quote treatment, and that is worth being honest about. It is a real inspection with a written report either way, and there is no obligation.

Will one treatment fix it?

For wasps, usually. For mice, only if the entry is sealed. For German cockroaches, no — that takes a sequence over several weeks, and anyone promising a single visit is selling you a relapse.

Do you handle wildlife?

Squirrels, raccoons and bats in the structure, yes — exclusion and one-way doors, not poison. Bats have legal protections and a maternity season when they cannot be excluded at all; we work to that calendar.
