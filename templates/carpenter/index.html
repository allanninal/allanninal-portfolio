Recent Built-Ins

Six built-ins, and what was out of square in each.

Every carpenter's website is a lightbox of finished woodwork. It is beautiful and it is useless, because you cannot see the hard part in a photograph — the hard part was that the wall leaned, the floor fell away an inch and the radiator could not move. So each of these names the defect in the room and the joinery decision that absorbed it.

  • Heritage Hill · 1908 foursquare · Paired alcove bookcases, 8'2" tall

    Two alcoves either side of a chimney breast, neither the same width

    Room
    The two alcoves differed by 3⁄8" in width and the east wall was out of plumb 5⁄8" top to bottom. The plaster bellied out at picture-rail height on one side only.
    Species & stock
    Paint-grade poplar face frames, birch ply carcases, Michigan hard maple for the adjustable shelves.
    Joint
    Face frames dominoed and built 3⁄8" narrow, then scribed to the plaster on site with a compass and a block plane. Carcases set plumb and independent of the frames; nothing nailed through the front.
    Finish
    Sprayed in the shop — two coats of pigmented conversion varnish over a bonding primer. Site work was scribe stock and caulk only.

    Built for $9,400

  • Eastown · 1921 bungalow · Window seat with three drawers and a hinged lid

    A window seat over a floor that fell away an inch to the bay

    Room
    The floor dropped a full 1" from the room side to the bay over 62 inches, and the bay itself was 3⁄4" out of square corner to corner. Both original casings had to stay.
    Species & stock
    Quartersawn white oak, solid for the lid and the front rail, rift-sawn ply for the ends.
    Joint
    Levelled off the high corner and the plinth scribed down to the floor, so the lid reads level and the taper hides in the base shadow line. Drawer boxes are half-blind dovetailed and hung on undermount slides that tolerate the racking.
    Finish
    Hardwax oil, three coats, rubbed back between. Repairable in place by the owner, which matters on a lid people sit on.

    Built for $6,800

  • East Hills · 1913 Craftsman · Four-bay lockers with a bench, hooks and a boot tray

    Mudroom lockers on a wall that leaned into the room

    Room
    The back wall leaned 7⁄8" into the room over 84 inches, and the return wall was 2° off ninety. An unmovable dryer vent came through 11 inches above the bench height.
    Species & stock
    Paint-grade poplar, birch ply carcases, solid hard maple bench top and hook rail.
    Joint
    Built as four independent bays with 1" scribe stock on both stiles and a filler at the return, so the lean is absorbed across the run rather than dumped in one gap. The vent got a removable panel rather than a hole cut in a finished side.
    Finish
    Conversion varnish on the casework; the maple bench top in hardwax oil so it can be sanded and re-oiled when the boots have had their way with it.

    Built for $7,900

  • Ottawa Hills · 1926 foursquare · Full-height pantry with pull-outs and a counter run

    A pantry in a room that had been three rooms

    Room
    Three different plaster thicknesses along one 11-foot wall where two doorways had been closed up at different dates. Nothing was coplanar; the worst step was 1⁄2".
    Species & stock
    Cherry face frames and doors, birch ply carcases with cherry veneer where the interiors show.
    Joint
    A shadow-gap reveal down the whole run instead of a scribed edge — when a wall changes plane three times, a scribe just chases the mess. The reveal is a deliberate 3⁄8" line and reads as design.
    Finish
    Clear conversion varnish, satin. Cherry darkens for about eighteen months and then holds; the doors were kept out of the sun in the shop so it darkens evenly.

    Built for $14,200

  • Cherry Hill · 1917 four-square · Media wall with flanking cabinets and floating shelves

    A media wall around a chimney that was not centred on the room

    Room
    The chimney breast sat 4 1⁄2" off the room centreline, which nobody notices until something symmetrical is built against it. Ceiling dropped 5⁄8" left to right.
    Species & stock
    Walnut floating shelves; paint-grade poplar and birch ply for the cabinetry below.
    Joint
    Cabinets built to different widths so the openings match rather than the boxes — the eye reads the openings. Crown scribed to the falling ceiling and stopped short at both ends so the drop never meets a wall.
    Finish
    Cabinets sprayed in conversion varnish; the walnut shelves in hardwax oil. Walnut was kept off the window wall on purpose — it fades in direct sun and nobody wants that surprise.

    Built for $11,600

  • Baxter · 1911 worker cottage · Three pull-out drawers and a hinged access door

    Under-stair storage where the stringer was the only straight line

    Room
    A raking triangle with a 33.5° stringer, a wall that bowed 1⁄2" at mid-height and a stair carriage nobody was allowed to touch. Headroom ran out before the third drawer did.
    Species & stock
    Birch ply throughout, paint-grade poplar face frame, hard maple drawer runners.
    Joint
    The face frame is a single scribed template taken off the actual rake with a profile gauge, not off a measurement. Drawers hang from the top so the bowed wall carries no load and the third one is deliberately shallower.
    Finish
    Two coats of pigmented conversion varnish. Interiors left in clear-coated birch so the space reads bigger than it is.

    Built for $4,300

Pieces & Prices

What we build, and what it costs.

“Call for pricing” is the single biggest bounce on a custom-work site. You do not know whether an alcove bookcase is $1,200 or $12,000, and rather than guess you leave. So here are the real ranges, with the two or three things that actually move them. Ranges are supplied-and-installed, in Kent and Ottawa counties, at 2026 prices.

    Alcove bookcases

    $3,800 – $11,000

    per alcove, supplied and installed

    The classic pre-war built-in: floor-to-ceiling or capped at the picture rail, in the recess beside a chimney breast. Almost always a pair, and almost never a matching pair, because the two recesses are rarely the same width.

The Honest Comparison

Built-in, or bought-in.

For most jobs the real rival is not another carpenter, it is a stock cabinet run plus trim at a third of the price. Pretending that does not exist is what makes a joinery site read as evasive. Here is where each one wins, including the three cases where you should keep your money.

  • Build it

    A recess that is out of plumb or out of square

    This is the whole commercial reason the trade exists. A factory carcase is a rectangular prism; the recess is not. Stock boxes plus scribe trim in a leaning alcove give you a 1" wedge of painted filler at eye height and it is the only thing anyone will see.

  • Buy the boxes

    A straight run against a square wall

    Buy the boxes. If the wall is flat and plumb and the run is uninterrupted, a stock carcase run with a decent face frame and good trim gets you 90% of the look for about a third of the money. We will tell you this on the survey rather than after the quote.

  • Build it

    A bay window or a dormer

    Three walls, none of them at the angle on the plan, plus a floor that has usually settled toward the bay. There is no stock product for this shape and there never has been.

  • Buy the boxes

    A rental or a flip

    A built-in is a fixture; you cannot take it with you and the next owner may not want it. On a property you are selling inside two years the money is better spent almost anywhere else.

  • Build it

    Non-standard heights: coved ceilings, picture rails, deep skirting

    Stock carcases come in a few heights and none of them land on a 1918 picture rail. The filler panel above a stock run with a nine-foot-two ceiling is the tell that makes a whole kitchen look bought.

  • Buy the boxes

    A room you will re-do within five years

    Custom joinery is priced on the assumption it stays. If the layout is provisional, keep it provisional and spend the difference when the layout is not.

If your job is one of the bought-in three, we will tell you at the survey rather than after you have paid for drawings, and we will give you the name of a fitter who does that work well. We would rather lose the job than build you something you did not need.

Materials

Wood, joints, and a Michigan winter.

Paint-grade versus stain-grade is the fork that moves a quote two to three times over, and most people have never heard the words. Wood movement is the other one: it is a real constraint, not a flourish, and it is the reason a gap that opens in February is a design decision rather than a defect.

  • Paint-grade or stain-grade

    The fork that moves the number two to three times over.

    Paint-grade means poplar, birch ply and MDF: stable, cheap, sprays beautifully, and the grain is nobody’s business because it is under two coats of pigment. Stain-grade means the timber is the finish — every board is selected for figure and colour, offcuts you would happily use in paint-grade become firewood, and the yield drops. That yield is the cost. It is not a markup for prettiness.

  • Michigan hardwood, because it is a day’s drive away

    Hard maple, cherry, white oak and walnut, in that order of use.

    Hard maple for shelving and bench tops because it takes a beating. Cherry when someone wants warmth and will accept that it darkens for eighteen months before it settles. Quartersawn white oak for anything in a Craftsman house, because it is what is already in the room. Walnut for a shelf or a lid, and never on a sunny window wall, because it fades toward grey in direct light and that surprise is not one we hand out.

  • What a Michigan winter does to a panel

    ~65% relative humidity in August, ~25% in a heated house in January.

    Across that swing a 12" solid panel moves about 1⁄8" across the grain, and nothing you do stops it. So panels float in their grooves rather than being glued in, face frames are engineered to take the movement at the joints, and a built-in installed in July gets scribed differently from one installed in January. This is ordinary shop knowledge; we state it because a gap that appears in February is a design decision, not a defect, and you should know which one you are looking at.

  • Solid, veneer and ply in the same piece

    A good built-in usually uses all three, deliberately.

    Solid timber where it is seen edge-on and takes wear: face frames, lids, bench tops, shelf fronts. Veneered ply for wide flat panels, because a 24"-wide solid panel wants to move a quarter of an inch and ply does not. Plain birch ply for carcase backs and interiors nobody will see. A shop that uses solid everywhere is not being generous, it is building something that will crack.

  • The finish is a schedule, not a colour

    Conversion varnish, hardwax oil, and which one you can repair yourself.

    Conversion varnish is sprayed in the shop, cures hard, and is what makes painted casework look factory rather than brushed. It cannot be spot-repaired at home. Hardwax oil is softer, feels like timber rather than plastic, and can be sanded and re-oiled in place by the owner with a rag — which is why bench tops and lids get it and cabinet doors do not. We spray in the shop rather than on site, so your house does not become a spray booth.

How It Goes

From template to install day.

Six steps, and the one that matters most is the fourth: the point at which the number stops moving. Custom work has a reputation for open-ended invoices and it is earned, so this page says exactly where that line sits.

  1. 1

    A visit, and a straightedge

    An hour at the house. We measure, but more usefully we find out how far out the room is: a long level on the walls, a profile gauge on the rake, diagonals across the bay. That is the survey that decides whether this is a custom job at all.

  2. 2

    A drawing and a range

    A dimensioned elevation and a price range, not a number. If a stock run plus trim would serve you better, this is where we say so, and we would rather say it now than after you have paid for drawings.

  3. 3

    Species, finish and hardware chosen

    Paint-grade or stain-grade, the species, the finish schedule and the runners. These are the four decisions that move the price, and they all get made here rather than halfway through the build.

  4. 4

    The number is fixed here

    You sign the drawing and the specification, and the price stops moving. After this point a change is a variation with its own number, in writing. Nothing is cut before this and everything is cut after it, which is exactly why the line sits where it does.

  5. 5

    Built and finished in the shop

    Four to seven weeks on the bench depending on the piece. It is sprayed or oiled here, not in your living room, so what arrives on install day is finished work with only scribe stock left proud.

  6. 6

    Install day, and the scribing

    One to three days on site. This is where the leaning wall gets dealt with: compass, block plane, a lot of offering up. Then caulk, touch-up, and we take the packaging away with us.

Hi, I’m Ruth.

I served my time in a millwork shop on the west side making store fixtures, which is where you learn that nothing on a job site is the dimension the drawing says it is. Foursquare is two benches and an installer. I draw every piece, I cut most of it, and I am there on install day for anything that has to be scribed — which is all of it, because Grand Rapids housing stock is 1900 to 1930 and there is not a plumb wall in Heritage Hill. The work I like best is the alcove nobody thinks can be used: the one beside the chimney breast that runs out five-eighths of an inch over nine feet and has a radiator pipe coming through the floor at the wrong end.

Everything is drawn before it is cut and templated before it is drawn. On a wall that leans, a measurement is a guess: I take the actual line off the plaster with a profile gauge and a story stick, and the face frame is built to that rather than to a number on a pad. It is slower at the front of the job and it is the reason install day is one to three days instead of a fortnight of packers and caulk.

We build built-ins and nothing else. Not furniture, not whole kitchens, not trim carpentry for builders. Doing one thing is why the lead time is eight to ten weeks rather than six months, and why I can tell you within an hour of walking into your house whether this is a job worth doing at all.

What does an alcove bookcase actually cost?
Between about $3,800 and $11,000 per alcove, supplied and installed. The spread is real, not evasive: paint-grade poplar capped at the picture rail with an open plinth sits near the bottom, and stain-grade white oak running to a coved ceiling with doors and drawers in the base sits near the top. The four things that move it are paint-grade versus stain-grade, height, doors versus open, and how far out of plumb the recess is. We publish ranges because a homeowner who cannot tell whether this is a $1,200 job or a $12,000 job usually just leaves.
What is your lead time?
We are currently quoting installs eight to ten weeks out. That covers drawings and sign-off, four to seven weeks on the bench, and the finishing schedule, which cannot be rushed without it showing. If you need something for a specific date, tell us at the survey and we will say yes or no then rather than in week six.
Would stock cabinets do the same job for less?
Sometimes, and we would rather say so at the survey. For a straight run against a flat, plumb wall, a stock carcase run with a decent face frame and good trim gets you most of the look for about a third of the money. Custom earns its price where the room is out of square: alcoves, bays, rakes, non-standard heights, and any wall that leans. If your job is the first kind, we will tell you and give you the name of a good fitter.
How much deposit do you take?
A third on signing the drawing and specification, a third when the carcases are built, and the balance on completion of the install. The first payment is what puts materials on the bench; the last one is not due until you have walked the piece with us.
Will the install damage my plaster?
Some, honestly. Fixings go into studs, and a scribe against a bellied wall sometimes needs the high spot knocked back. We patch and make good what we disturb, but we do not redecorate the room, and on a lath-and-plaster wall over a hundred years old we will always agree the fixing positions with you before anything goes in.
A gap has appeared in a panel since the winter. Is that a fault?
Almost certainly not. Grand Rapids goes from around 65% relative humidity in August to about 25% inside a heated house in January, and across that swing a 12-inch solid panel moves roughly an eighth of an inch across the grain. Panels are built to float for exactly this reason, so what you are seeing is the design working. If a joint has opened, or a door has dropped, that is different and it is covered.
Do you need my house empty, and for how long?
No. The build happens at the shop. Install is one to three days depending on the piece, in one room, with dust sheets and a vacuum running. The only thing we ask is clear access to the wall and somewhere to set up a bench, ideally a garage.
Can you match the existing trim in an old house?
Usually. We take a profile gauge to your casing or skirting and cut a matching knife if the run justifies it, or find the closest stock profile and tell you honestly how close it is. On quartersawn oak in a Craftsman house, matching the ray fleck matters more than matching the profile, and that is a timber selection problem rather than a machining one.
Do you do kitchens, furniture, or trim carpentry?
Built-ins only. Freestanding furniture is a different discipline and a different shop; whole kitchens want a designer and a fitter as well as a cabinetmaker; trim carpentry is a subcontract relationship with builders rather than something a homeowner buys. Doing one thing is why the lead time is eight to ten weeks instead of six months.
What does the five-year warranty actually cover?
The joinery and the fixings: a joint that opens, a carcase that racks, a door that drops out of alignment, a drawer that stops running. It does not cover seasonal movement in a solid panel, which is normal and is described in the wood guide, and it does not cover finish damage from use. Hardware carries its own manufacturer warranty, which we register in your name.
