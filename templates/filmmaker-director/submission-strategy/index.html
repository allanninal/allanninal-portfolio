Submission Strategy.

The demo publishes what one run cost. This is the method that would have made it cost less — and the two withdrawals in that ledger are both failures of the premiere section below, which is why it is here rather than in a blog post nobody reads.

Tier the list before you spend anything

Tier 1

The eight or ten everybody has heard of

$65–110 · hit rate Under 1%

Spend: Two, maybe three, and only on the early deadline

A tier-1 selection genuinely changes what happens next. Fifteen tier-1 submissions is not a strategy, it is a lottery ticket bought fifteen times.

Tier 2

Regional festivals with real programmers and real audiences

$30–60 · hit rate 3–8%

Spend: Most of the budget

This is where a short film actually lives. The rooms are smaller and the people in them are the ones who commission work.

Tier 3

Small, local, or genre-specific

$15–30 · hit rate 15–30%

Spend: Enough to guarantee the film screens somewhere

Screening in front of thirty people beats screening in front of nobody, and a filmmaker who has never watched an audience watch their film is missing the only feedback that is not an opinion.

Never

Festivals that exist to collect fees

Any · hit rate High and worthless

Spend: Nothing

Signs: no physical venue, an "official selection" for almost everyone, laurels emailed within days, and a submission platform listing with hundreds of awards and no photographs of a room.

What a waiver is worth

  1. ● "We can offer you a fee waiver." Take it Usually genuine and usually worth taking — it costs the festival nothing and signals a programmer already wants the film.
  2. – "Waiver code: submit by Friday." Neutral A marketing push to hit a submission target. Neutral. Take it if the festival was already on your list and ignore it if it was not.
  3. ? "Free to submit, $60 if selected." Ask first A screening fee. Legitimate at some venues and a red flag at most. Ask what the $60 pays for and whether the audience is ticketed.
  4. × "Guaranteed selection with the premium tier." Not a festival Not a festival. This is a laurel shop and the laurel is worth exactly what it cost.

Premiere status disqualifies more films than quality does

Four rules, and a film can fall foul of any of them without anybody having watched it.

World premiere
Has never screened publicly anywhere, ever.
Spending it is the single largest strategic decision of a run. It can only be spent once, and most people spend it in month one on a festival that did not require it.
Regional premiere
First screening in that state, province or territory.
Cheap to preserve and easy to destroy by accident. Two festivals four hours apart in the same month can disqualify each other.
Online premiere
Has never been publicly viewable online, password-free.
Putting the film on a public link during a run ends this permanently. A Vimeo password is not a technicality — it is the whole difference.
Local screenings
A cast-and-crew screening, a fundraiser, a class showing.
Read each festival’s definition. Some count a ticketed local screening as a public premiere and some do not, and the ones that do will not make an exception.

The deadline is the budget

Earlybird
$25–40
5–7 months out. The same film, the same programmers, and roughly half the price. This is the entire cost-control lever and it is a calendar problem, not a money problem.
Regular
$40–65
3–4 months out. Where most people submit, because most people finish late.
Late
$60–90
6–10 weeks out. No advantage of any kind beyond having more time to finish. Programmers do not see the deadline you used.
Extended
$75–120
Days out. A revenue exercise. If the film is not ready, the honest move is to wait a year — a short film does not expire, and it is the same film in March.

Forty submissions at the earlybird deadline is roughly $1,300. The same forty at the late deadline is roughly $2,900. Nothing about the film changed. Finishing eight weeks earlier is worth more than any other single decision on this page, and it is the one nobody frames as a budget decision.

6 ways a run wastes money

  1. 1 The world premiere spent in month one on a festival that never asked for it.
  2. 2 Fifteen tier-1 submissions and no tier-3 ones, so a finished film screens nowhere.
  3. 3 A public Vimeo link posted during the run, ending the online premiere for every festival left on the list.
  4. 4 Two neighbouring festivals in the same month, each disqualifying the other on regional premiere.
  5. 5 Everything submitted at the late deadline, at roughly double the fee, for no benefit whatsoever.
  6. 6 Fees tracked nowhere, so the true cost of the run is only discovered at tax time.

Fee ranges are typical of North American short-film festivals and move every year. Hit rates are one filmmaker's observed numbers across four films, not published statistics — almost no festival publishes theirs, which is itself worth noticing.
