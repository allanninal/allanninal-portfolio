Nobody publishes this. The laurel wall on every other site in this trade is the same list with thirty-five rows deleted — which is not dishonest exactly, but it does mean a first-time filmmaker looking at six laurels has no way to know whether that took six submissions or forty-one. It took forty-one. That is normal, and finding it out from a page rather than from fourteen months of your own money is the entire reason this table is here.

What the run was actually for

One screening led to work. No laurel did.

One screening led to work. Not one laurel did.

A producer in the audience at Greatlakes commissioned a six-minute piece four months later. She had not seen the laurel, the website or the trailer — she was in the room. Everything else the run produced was for me, not for anyone hiring.

One programmer now answers email.

The Quarry Lake note was three paragraphs about why the second act does not work, and it was right. A relationship with somebody who will read your next film honestly is the rarest thing a submission fee can buy, and it is not what the fee is advertised as buying.

The rest was a marketing budget with no attribution.

Thirty-five rejections and four withdrawals cost a real amount of money and produced no measurable outcome. I would spend it again, and I want to be precise about what it did and did not do rather than imply the laurels are a career.

The most useful thing the run produced

“The second act does not work — you cut away from her three times in ninety seconds and every one of them is you flinching. Everything either side of it is confident. Send me the next one.”

Programmer’s note, Quarry Lake Cinema, August 2025 — quoted with permission

Three paragraphs from a programmer at a festival with a $20 entry fee, and it was right. That email is on this page because it is worth more than four of the 6 selections and because printing your own bad review is the only way anyone can tell you are not curating.

What Hollow Ice cost

$7,370 to make. $8,960 to make and run.

Two totals, because the $1,590 of entry fees is the line almost every “how I made my short” post leaves out — and leaving it out is how a $8,960 film gets described as a $7,370 one.

Common questions

Mostly about the ledger.

Why publish the rejections?

Because the alternative is a laurel wall, which is the same list with thirty-five rows deleted. Somebody making their first short and looking at six laurels has no way to know whether that took six submissions or forty-one. It took forty-one. Knowing that from a page is cheaper than finding it out from fourteen months of your own money.

Does a festival run get you work?

Once, in my case, and it was a person in a room rather than a laurel on a website. I would still do it, but I would not describe it as a business development strategy, and I would be suspicious of anyone who does.

Are those real festival names?

No. The shape of the record is real; the names are invented. Publishing a rejection record against named real festivals would be a different and much less defensible kind of honesty.

How do you choose where to submit?

Tiers, waivers and premiere status, in that order — and getting the third one wrong is what the two withdrawals in the ledger are. There is a longer method than fits in an answer here.

Will you read my script?

Yes, as a paid read, and you will get a written response inside a week rather than a coffee that never gets scheduled. If it is not for me I will say so in the first paragraph rather than the last.

Do you take commercial work?

Yes, and it paid for two of the four films on this page. I do not treat it as the thing I do while waiting to make films; it is how the films get made, which is a more honest framing than most directors offer.
