What the work actually is.

  1. Rig removal 46 h · 22 shots
    What you saw

    An actor lands from a two-storey drop and walks away.

    What was there

    A stunt performer on eleven visible wires, a crash mat, and two crew members holding a descender in frame.

    Wire paint is the single most common thing a compositor actually does, and the only evidence it happened is that you did not see any.

  2. Sky replacement 31 h · 14 shots
    What you saw

    A clear evening sky over the hills behind the house.

    What was there

    Flat white overcast, blown out two stops, with a contrail across the top third.

    Easy to do badly and very hard to do so nobody notices. The edges of hair and foliage are where it falls apart.

  3. Set extension 58 h · 9 shots
    What you saw

    A warehouse forty metres deep with racking to the ceiling.

    What was there

    Nine metres of practical set, a black drape, and a taped line on the floor for eyelines.

    The most expensive item on this list per shot, and the one clients are most often surprised to learn was there at all.

  4. Screen inserts 27 h · 31 shots
    What you saw

    A phone showing an app, held and tilted.

    What was there

    A phone with a green card taped to it, reflecting the ceiling lights, and a thumb over the corner.

    Sounds trivial and is not: the reflection, the moiré and the thumb are three separate problems.

  5. Crowd tiling 34 h · 6 shots
    What you saw

    Four thousand people in a stand.

    What was there

    Forty extras moved through six positions, shot six times from a locked-off camera.

    The work is not the duplication. It is making sure no two of the same person are ever visible in one frame.

  6. Object removal 22 h · 27 shots
    What you saw

    A period street with nothing modern in it.

    What was there

    Yellow lines, three satellite dishes, a wheelie bin and a coffee cup somebody left on a windowsill.

    The coffee cup is real and it happens on almost every job. Nobody is careless; a set is a busy place.

  7. Beauty and clean-up 19 h · 18 shots
    What you saw

    A face in close-up, lit well.

    What was there

    The same face, plus a boom shadow across one cheek and a continuity mark from an earlier setup.

    Kept deliberately conservative, and the line where it stops is a conversation worth having before the shoot rather than in the grade.

  8. Split screens 41 h · 12 shots
    What you saw

    Two characters in one continuous take, one of them played by the same actor.

    What was there

    Two passes on a motion-control move, with a lighting change between them nobody noticed on the day.

    Almost always cheaper than the alternative, and almost never budgeted for because it does not look like an effect.

  9. Stabilisation and retime 29 h · 16 shots
    What you saw

    A smooth push in, at the right speed.

    What was there

    A handheld take with a bump at frame 60, running about 15% too fast for the cut.

    A retime is not a slider. Anything with motion blur has to be rebuilt, and that is where the hours go.

Multipliers against the same shot

The plate decides the price.

The largest cost driver in visual effects is not the shot. It is what the camera department did or did not do on the day, and a client who reads this table before a shoot saves more money than any negotiation afterwards can recover.

What the camera department did, and what it costs in post
On the day Cost Why
Tracking markers, correctly placed ×1.0 The baseline. Twenty minutes of tape on the day, and the track solves itself.
Locked-off camera, no markers ×1.2 Nothing to track. Still needs a clean plate, which is thirty seconds of rolling on an empty set.
Clean plate shot ×0.7 The only row that makes a shot cheaper. Roll the empty frame before or after the take and the removal work roughly halves.
Lens and camera data recorded ×0.9 Focal length, focus distance, height and tilt. Written down, it is free; reverse-engineered, it is a day.
Handheld, no markers ×4.0 Every feature has to be hand-tracked frame by frame. This is the single most expensive omission on any set.
Greenscreen lit unevenly ×2.5 A hot spot and a shadow mean two keys and a hand-drawn garbage matte per shot, per frame range.
Hair or foliage against greenscreen ×3.0 Spill on fine detail. Nothing that happens in post fixes what a backlight would have fixed on the day.
Motion blur through the removal area ×2.0 The thing being removed is smeared across what stays. There is no clean edge to work to.

The spread between “clean plate shot” at ×0.7 and “handheld, no markers” at ×4.0 is a factor of 5.7, on the same shot, for decisions that each take under a minute on set.

None of this is a complaint about camera departments, who are solving a different set of problems under more pressure. It is an argument for having somebody from post in the room for one hour before the shoot, which is the cheapest hour on any production.

Versions

v40 is a healthy shot.

  1. v1 – v8

    Technical. Track, roto, keys. Nothing anybody would show a client yet.

  2. v9 – v20

    The shot exists and is wrong in interesting ways. Most client notes land here and most of them are right.

  3. v21 – v34

    Integration. Grain, edges, light wrap, the reasons a good composite stops looking like two images.

  4. v35 – v40+

    Notes from people who have now watched it two hundred times, and a final that differs from v35 in ways only the four of us can see.

A client who reads "v37" in a filename and hears thrashing will start asking for fewer iterations, and fewer iterations is the one thing guaranteed to make the shot worse. Forty versions on a hero shot is a healthy shot. Six is a shot nobody looked at.

Common questions

Starting with the missing reel.

Why is there no reel on this page?

There is one, and it goes out in the first email. It is also the least honest document in this trade — mine included — because it is necessarily made of the small fraction of the work that is allowed to be visible. The list on this page is the other 90%.

Can you fix it in post?

Usually yes, and the plate table is what it costs. The question worth asking is not whether it can be fixed but whether one hour of planning before the shoot would have made it free, and for most of that table the answer is yes.

How many versions should we expect?

Around forty on a hero shot, and that is a healthy number rather than a worrying one. Read the versions section — a shot that arrives at v6 is a shot nobody has really looked at.

Do you do CG, or only compositing?

Compositing, clean-up and integration, plus straightforward CG elements. For a creature or a full CG environment I will tell you that on the first call and point you somewhere better rather than learning on your budget.

What do you need from us on the day?

Markers, a clean plate, and the lens data written down. Those three things move most shots from the bottom half of the plate table to the top, and all three are free.

Can we see the before and after?

Yes, and it is genuinely the most persuasive thing I have — but only after the fact. Before you hire anybody, the useful comparison is not a breakdown video; it is whether they can tell you in advance what your plates are going to cost.
