It never stops the way a fired pot does

Wood movement

Cellulose is hygroscopic: a board's width changes with the season, for the life of the piece — not once, like a kiln firing, but continuously.

The formula

width × (species shrinkage % ÷ fiber-saturation point) × Δ moisture content — tangential shrinkage (parallel to the growth rings, the wide face of most boards) runs roughly 6–10% green-to-oven-dry; radial shrinkage (through the rings) runs about half that. Longitudinal movement, along the grain, is under 0.1% and treated as zero.

Worked example: the tabletop

Run against The Blue Ridge Dining Table's widest panel — the 42″ top — using white oak's own published tangential-shrinkage coefficient, not a rounded-off "solid wood moves" shrug.

What holds, and why

Joinery

Five terms, what each does, and what fails without it.

Mortise & tenon
A tongue (tenon) cut on one part fits a matching slot (mortise) cut into another — the basic structural joint of almost every piece on this page. Fails without it: the joint relies on glue alone, and glue alone doesn’t survive a chair or table’s racking stress.
Breadboard end
A narrow strip capping the end grain of a wide panel, pinned (not glued) so the panel can move under it — the joint that lets a tabletop widen and narrow with the seasons without splitting. Fails without it: end grain left exposed checks and cups within a year or two.
Drawbore
A mortise-and-tenon pin hole bored slightly offset between the two parts, so driving the peg pulls the joint tight without clamps or glue. Fails without it: a glued-only tenon can creep loose over decades of seasonal movement; a drawbored one mechanically can’t.
Through-tenon
A tenon cut long enough to pass completely through the receiving part and show on the far face, often wedged there. Fails without it (i.e., a blind tenon in its place): less glue surface and less mechanical lock on a stretcher or leg that takes real load.
Dovetail
Interlocking trapezoidal pins and tails cut into two case corners, mechanically resisting the pulling-apart force a loaded drawer or case side puts on a joint. Fails without it: a butt-jointed or nailed case corner works loose exactly where the load is highest.

Hardwax oil vs. conversion varnish

Applying the finish, in order

  1. Sand through the grits Through 120, then 150, then 180 grit, raising the grain between the last two passes.
  2. Tack off the dust Wipe down with a tack cloth — no sanding dust caught in the grain lines before the first coat.
  3. Apply the first coat Work hardwax oil into the grain with a lint-free applicator, end grain last.
  4. Buff off the excess After 15–20 minutes, buff off every trace of standing oil — what’s left on the surface is what streaks.
  5. Let it dry 24 hours before recoating — see the finish schedule above.
  6. Apply the second coat Same method, then buff again.
  7. Full cure 7 days before the piece sees normal use — water and cleaning wait until then.

Caring for solid wood

  1. Wipe spills immediately A soft, dry cloth — standing liquid is what actually damages a finish, not the spill itself.
  2. Use coasters and trivets Under anything hot, wet or cold, every time.
  3. Hold indoor humidity steady 35–55% relative humidity year-round where you can — this is what the wood-movement math above is computed against.
  4. Dust dry or barely damp Skip silicone- or wax-based sprays; they build up and complicate a future re-coat.
  5. Expect the breadboard ends to move A little seasonal give at the ends is the joint doing its job, not a defect — don’t clamp or glue it shut.
  6. Re-oil worn areas yearly Hardwax oil only; conversion varnish doesn’t need this step. See the finish schedule above for which this piece has.

Commission process & lead time

How a commission moves

  1. Inquiry Piece type, rough dimensions and timeline, shared through the form below.
  2. Design & shop drawing Adaeze drafts the to-scale elevation you see above this page’s cut list, sized to your piece.
  3. 50% deposit Due on approval of the drawing — this is what starts the clock on lead time, not the inquiry.
  4. Material selection & cut list Species and sawing method confirmed per part; the cut list — and its board footage — comes from the approved drawing, not before.
  5. Build Joinery cut, dry-fit, glued up, inside the lead-time band for your piece’s size.
  6. Finish & cure Hardwax oil or conversion varnish applied per the finish schedule, then cured before delivery.
  7. Delivery & install Balance due on delivery, within about a day’s drive of Asheville.

FAQ

Questions

Why can’t you quote a price today?

Because lumber is priced and sold by the board foot, and board footage doesn’t exist until the cut list does — which doesn’t exist until the shop drawing is approved. See the cut list above for exactly how that arithmetic runs once it can.

Will my table really move with the seasons?

Yes, by a small, computable amount — see the wood-movement worked example above. That’s exactly why solid tops ship with breadboard ends, floating panels or elongated screw slots instead of being glued rigid across the grain.

Can I bring my own wood?

Sometimes — if it’s kiln-dried to 6–8% moisture content and there’s enough of it, net of the cut list plus 15–20% waste. Raise it at the inquiry stage so it can be checked before the drawing is drafted.

Do you deliver and install outside Asheville?

Yes, within about a day’s drive. Delivery and install are quoted separately once the piece is built, based on distance — the same “not until it’s real” approach as the piece itself.
