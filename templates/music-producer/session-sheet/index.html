Session Sheet.

The demo splits the credit into four jobs. This is the paperwork behind the two that happen in a room. None of it is clever — all of it is the difference between a tracking day that ends at six and one that ends at nine.

Input list — 12 lines

Channel, source, microphone, pre-amp, and why
ChSourceMicPreWhy
1 Kick in Dynamic, inside the port Clean Phase-checked against the outside mic before anything is recorded, not after.
2 Kick out Large dynamic, at the head Clean Where the body is. Half of the finished kick usually comes from this one.
3 Snare top Dynamic, off-axis to the hats Clean Angle matters more than model. Point it away from the hi-hat and half the mix problem is gone.
4 Snare bottom Small condenser Clean Polarity flipped at the desk and labelled as flipped, so nobody flips it twice.
5–6 Overheads Matched pair Clean Measured to the snare with a cable so the centre is actually centred.
7 Room Ribbon, back of the room Coloured The one channel worth over-committing on. It is the sound of the record.
8 Bass DI — Clean Always taken, even when the amp is the sound. It costs a cable and saves a re-amp session.
9 Bass amp Dynamic, off-centre cone Coloured Off-centre for less beam and more body.
10–11 Electric gtr Dynamic + ribbon, same speaker Coloured Two mics, same speaker, capsules aligned. Blended at mix, not on the day.
12 Acoustic Small condenser at the 12th fret Clean Twelfth fret, a hand-span back. Not over the hole, whatever the internet says.
13 Lead vocal Large condenser Coloured Compressed lightly on the way in — 3 dB at most, so the mix has somewhere to go.
14 Vocal safety Dynamic, same stand Clean A second capture at a lower gain. It has rescued two records and cost one channel.

Headphones

Five mixes, built before anybody plays a note. The day stalls here more often than it stalls anywhere else.

Drummer
Click loud, bass loud, everything else quiet
Timing is their whole job. A drummer who cannot hear the click will drift, and a drummer who cannot hear the bass will fight it.
Bass
Kick and their own DI, click medium
They are locking to the kick, not to the room.
Guitar
Vocal up, drums medium, own amp low
They hear their own amp in the room anyway, and playing to the vocal is what makes an arrangement sit.
Vocalist
Themselves loud, some reverb, everything else balanced
Reverb in the cans is not a mix decision, it is a performance one. It is free and it changes what you get.
Everyone
A talkback that is always live in the control room
The single biggest time saver in a tracking day. Every second spent asking somebody to press a button is spent twice.

The take log

Five columns, filled in as you go
Column What goes in it Why
Take number Sequential, never reset Reusing a number across a day is how the wrong take reaches the mix.
Complete? Y / N / partial A partial with a great chorus is worth more than three complete mediocre ones, and only the log knows.
Keeper marks Bar ranges Written while it is fresh. "The second verse in take 7" is a sentence nobody remembers on Thursday.
Comment Five words "Rushed the turnaround" or "best feel". Enough to comp from without listening to everything again.
Comp source Take per section The comp is a decision, and a decision that is not written down gets remade badly at 11pm.

Handover

  1. 01 Consolidated stems, bar 1 To the spec on the public page. Exported at the end of the tracking day, not the week the mix starts.
  2. 02 The rough mix However bad. It carries the intent, and no set of stems does.
  3. 03 The take log So the mixer knows which parts were chosen deliberately and which survived by accident.
  4. 04 The input list Mic, pre-amp and position per channel. It explains why a channel sounds the way it does before anybody EQs it.
  5. 05 Tempo map and key A number, not "about 120". Anything time-based downstream depends on it.
  6. 06 A note on the mix bus If you were listening through a chain, say what it was. Do not print it.

6 ways a tracking day loses an evening

  1. 1 No input list, and a mix engineer guessing why the snare has no top end.
  2. 2 Headphone mixes built once, for the drummer, and four people playing to the wrong balance all day.
  3. 3 Take numbers reset after lunch, and the wrong verse comped in.
  4. 4 Comping decisions made at 11pm and never written down, then remade differently on Monday.
  5. 5 Stems exported three weeks later from a session that has since been "tidied".
  6. 6 A mix bus chain printed into the stems because it sounded good on the day.

The input list is for a five-piece tracking live in one room. A programmed record, a solo singer-songwriter or an orchestral date each need a different sheet, and borrowing one from the wrong kind of session is worse than writing a short one from scratch.
