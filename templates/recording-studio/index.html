A studio is a building.

Every studio site opens with a gear list. None of them tell you whether your band fits in the room, whether the organ gets up the stairs, or what happens to a take when the air handling has to be off in August. This one is drawn to scale and answers those first.

Will you fit? The load-in

Floor 3 · 12 ft 6 in to the beams

456 square feet, drawn to scale.

Drawn to scale from a tape measure, not from the lease. The live room is 456 square feet with a 12 ft 6 in ceiling, which is the single number that decides whether a loud kit sounds like a kit or like a room fighting one. The dashed line inside it is four feet from every wall: get closer than that and a source starts hearing the wall rather than the room. Everything below is generated from the same measurements.

Will you fit?

Same outline, same scale, same 4-foot clearance line as the plan above. Anything drawn crossing that line is inside four feet of a wall. 2 of the 8 arrangements below do not fit, and saying which is more useful than another photograph of a microphone.

Every arrangement asked for in nine years, including the ones I turn down
ArrangementVerdictThe constraint
Four-piece band, live Fits The default, and everything sits inside the four-foot clearance. Everyone has sightlines to the kit without headphones and the amps are in two different rooms, so the bleed is a choice rather than an accident.
String quartet Fits Everybody clears the walls by more than the four feet the room needs. The mains go up at nine feet, which the 12 ft 6 in ceiling allows and a ten-foot ceiling would not.
Fourteen-voice choir Does not fit It does not fit, and the reason is depth rather than floor area — there is plenty of floor. Three rows plus a conductor is about 17 feet front to back in a room that has 11 feet between its clearance lines, so the back row and the conductor both end up inside four feet of a wall and sound exactly like it. Nine voices is the honest ceiling here. The Methodist church on Haywood is a better room for this and I will make the call.
Solo acoustic and vocal Fits Guitar and voice together in the live room, two mics, no headphones. This is the room at its best and it is also the cheapest way to use it.
Drums alone, loud Fits What the 12 ft 6 in ceiling is for. Room mics at 11 feet in the far corner are most of the sound and they are free.
Full horn section, five Fits, tight Fits with the section standing in an arc and the trombone slide pointing into the room rather than at a wall. Book the half day, not the two-hour.
Nine-piece with kit Fits, tight The absolute ceiling. Amps go to the booth and the lounge, which means two players cannot see the drummer, which means a click. Say so when you book.
Grand piano Does not fit There is not one, and there will not be. A 7-foot grand is 60 inches across the case and the landing turn is 58. It has been measured twice by two different piano movers.

Kerb to live room · 34 treads · one turn

The landing is the constraint, not the door.

Measured corner to corner at 58 inches, because a diagonal is what a flight case actually travels. Everything wider than the landing has to come apart, go up on its side, or stay in the van.

  1. — The kerb Two loading spaces outside from 9am, unmetered until noon. After noon it is the deck on Rankin, which is 90 yards and a slope.
  2. 35 in Street door Propped open with a wedge that lives behind it. This is the widest thing on the route and it is not the constraint.
  3. — The stairs 34 treads in two flights with a turn. Nothing longer than about six feet gets round the turn upright, which is why the answer to the organ question is no.
  4. 58 in The landing The constraint. Measured corner to corner, not wall to wall, because that is the diagonal a case actually travels.
  5. 30 in Live room door Doors come off their hinges in about four minutes if something needs the extra inch and a half. It has been done nine times.

Three things and how they went

  • — Hammond B3 with bench 44 in wide and 425 lb. It has been up twice, both times with four people and a stair dolly, and both times I said never again. The Nord is in the control room for a reason.
  • — Leslie 122 Came up on its side and went back down the same way. Fine, but budget forty minutes at each end and do not book it against a hard out.
  • — 7-foot grand Does not go round the turn. Not "difficult" — does not go.

Measured, not modelled

What the building does to a take.

A room has neighbours, a roof, air handling and a bakery. None of that is fixable with panelling, so it is published instead.

Six facts about the building, four of which are constraints
MeasurementReadingWhat it means for a session
Noise floor, air handling off 19 dBA Measured at the live-room centre, C-weighted slow, at 3am with the building empty. This is the honest floor and it is why the air handling goes off.
Noise floor, air handling on 31 dBA Twelve decibels is the difference between a quiet room and a room. It runs between takes and not during them, which is a working method rather than a fault.

The list is two pages and it is not this

Four things change the record.

  • — The room A 456 sq ft timber floor at 0.62 seconds. It is the most expensive thing in the building and the only one that cannot be hired for the day.
  • — Two ribbon mics Coles 4038s as room pair. Ninety per cent of what people like about drums recorded here is these two microphones eleven feet up.
  • — A 1970s console Sixteen channels of it, used as a summing and preamp box rather than as a mixer. Recall is a photograph and a notebook, which is a real cost and worth it.
  • — Somebody who has been here nine years Knowing that the second window rattles at 94 Hz is not a spec. It is the difference between a usable take and a re-take, and it is not transferable to a different building.

There is a list — the usual preamps, the usual compressors, four more microphones than anybody needs, and a Nord because the organ does not come up the stairs. It is two pages, it is sent on request, and it has never once been the reason somebody booked. If a studio leads with its list, ask what its room measures.

A ten-hour day contains about six and a half hours of tape. The rest is load-in, setup, tuning, a tea break that everybody needs, and teardown. Any studio quoting an hourly rate against tracked minutes is quoting a number that has never existed.

Common questions

Mostly about the stairs.

Is the engineer included?

On the day and the half day, yes — that is me, for the whole booking, and there is no separate line. On a lockout, no: you bring your own and the price drops by $140 a day, which is the honest difference rather than a discount.

How loud can we be?

As loud as you like between 10am and 10pm, with two exceptions. Tuesdays and Thursdays from six to eight there is a dance class directly above and the footfall comes through the joists, so those hours are for overdubs and mixing. And nothing before ten, because the bakery below is a working business and the goodwill is worth more than the two hours.

Will our gear get up there?

Thirty-four treads, two flights, one turn with 58 inches of diagonal on the landing. Anything under six feet long or 33 inches wide goes up without drama. Beyond that, send me the dimensions before you book — I have measured the turn twice and I would rather tell you no by email than have you find out with a van outside.

Do you have a piano?

No, and there will not be one. A 7-foot grand is 60 inches across the case and the landing turn is 58. There is a Nord in the control room and an upright at the church on Haywood if a real piano is genuinely the sound, which I can arrange.

What actually happens in a ten-hour day?

Roughly: an hour of load-in and setup, forty minutes of drum tuning and mic placement, six and a half hours of tracking in blocks, an hour that disappears into lunch and one conversation about the second chorus, and the last forty minutes on rough mixes so you leave with something to listen to in the car.

Can we mix here later?

Yes, at the half-day rate, and it is usually a mistake to book it in the same week. The control room is not symmetrical, it is corrected rather than pretended about, and it is a better mix room on a Tuesday morning than it is at hour eleven of a tracking day.
