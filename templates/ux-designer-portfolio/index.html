The four, and who else was there

Named, not implied.

Patient intake, regional clinic group

2025 · Team of six · eleven weeks

The brief

Replace a paper intake form and a kiosk nobody used with something a person can finish in a waiting room on their own phone.

What shipped

A seven-screen flow, in production at nine sites. Median completion 4 min 20 s, down from a paper form that took 9 minutes and was 31% incomplete at the desk.

Front-end was Halvard Nyquist, two engineers. The visual language was already a design system I did not build; I chose from it and argued about two components.

Dispatch console, regional freight

2024 · Team of four · nine months, part time

The brief

One screen that eleven dispatchers look at for eight hours a day. The old one had 340 rows visible at once and everybody had built their own colour-coded system in a spreadsheet beside it.

What shipped

A density-first console with saved views. Still in use. The spreadsheets did not disappear and I have stopped thinking that was a failure.

Visual design was entirely Ines Bergqvist, who is the reason the thing is legible at 340 rows. I wrote CSS for the table layout and nothing else.

Catalogue search, county library system

2024 · Team of three · six weeks

The brief

Search that returned 900 results for “toni morrison” and put the large-print edition of a study guide first.

What shipped

Reworked ranking and a facet rail. The interesting part was not the interface — it was a metadata cleanup nobody wanted to fund and I did not do.

The ranking change was Marguerite Tolliver, a librarian who understood the catalogue better than any of us. I built the front end because there was nobody else, and it shows in two places.

Benefits enrolment, 900-person employer

2023 · Team of nine · fourteen weeks

The brief

An annual enrolment where 40% of staff picked the default because reading the alternative took an hour.

What shipped

A comparison step and plain-language summaries. Default-picking fell to 26%, and I do not know how much of that was the design and how much was HR emailing everybody twice.

Research was led by Anneke Prud’homme; I ran four of nineteen sessions. Visual design and build were an agency. Usability testing was run by their team and I watched.

Patient intake, regional clinic group

The prototype was wrong by week three.

A case study normally runs challenge, process, solution, which is the order the work is written up in and never the order it happens in. This is the order it happened in.

  1. What I believed in week one That the kiosk failed because it was ugly and slow, and that the same flow on a phone would fix it. I had a prototype by day four. It tested well with eight people in a room in an office.
  2. What the research actually found Eleven of nineteen patients were filling the form for somebody else — a parent, a partner, an adult child. The kiosk was not slow for them, it was wrong: every field said “you”.
  3. What that killed The prototype, and three weeks. Also the idea that completion time was the metric, since a proxy filling a form carefully takes longer and that is the good outcome.
  4. What we changed One question at the top: who is this for. It rewrites the pronouns in every subsequent field. It is four lines of logic and it is the entire project.
  5. What it cost Three weeks of my work and a difficult conversation with a product manager who had already told a board the date. We shipped two weeks late. The board did not notice; the desk staff did.
  6. What I would do differently Run the nineteen sessions before building the prototype rather than beside it. I knew that. I built it anyway because a prototype is how I think, and the honest fix is to build it and then not show anybody for a week.

Four things, and one of them is the whole job

What I need from you.

Five of your users, for an hour each
Not five stakeholders. Not a survey. Five people who use the thing, on a call, in the first two weeks. If that is genuinely impossible the engagement is still doable and everything I produce is a hypothesis, and I will label it as one throughout.
One person who can decide
In the room, or at least reachable in a day. A project with three decision-makers and no tiebreak does not produce a worse design; it produces the same design four times.
Read access to whatever you already measure
Analytics, support tickets, the spreadsheet somebody keeps. I would rather spend a day in your existing data than a week reproducing it badly.
Permission to ship something smaller
The single highest-value thing I can be given. Almost every project on this page got better when somebody agreed to cut a third of it, and none of them were scoped that way at the start.

Three weeks before anything is drawn.

  1. Week 0 — Nothing is designed Reading. Support tickets, existing analytics, whatever research already exists and got shelved. Most organisations have already been told the answer once.
  2. Weeks 1–2 — Still nothing is designed The five conversations. Recorded with permission, transcribed, and quoted rather than summarised — a summary is where a research finding goes to become whatever the reader already believed.
  3. Week 3 — One page What I think is true, what I am unsure of, and what I would need to find out. Reviewed with the decision-maker before anything is drawn. This is the meeting that decides whether the project works.
  4. Weeks 4–7 — Design, out loud Flows, then screens, in public — a shared file with everything in it including the discarded versions. No reveals. A reveal is a way of avoiding a conversation.
  5. Weeks 6–9 — Testing, in rounds of five Overlapping the design, not after it. Five people, three rounds, and the third round exists to check the fixes from the first two rather than to find new problems.
  6. Handover — A file and two hours Annotated states including the empty, error and loading ones, plus two hours with whoever builds it. The two hours are worth more than the annotations.

Common questions

Mostly about the matrix.

Why publish who did what? Nobody else does.

Because the first twenty minutes of every interview I have ever had were spent establishing it, politely and slowly. Putting it on the page gets that out of the way, and it means the things I did claim can be taken at face value. The cost is that the best-looking project on this site has “Not me” under visual design.

Is “Led” different from “Did”?

Led means I set the approach and other people worked to it. Did means I did the work, alone or nearly. Helped means somebody else owned it and I contributed real hours — not that I was in the meetings. If I was only in the meetings, the cell says “Not me”.

Why show flat results?

Because most results are flat, and a portfolio showing only the ones that moved is reporting a selection. The dispatch console reassignment time went from 41 seconds to 38 at n=11, which is noise, and I called it a win internally for a week before I looked at it properly.

What does “never measured” mean here?

That the question nobody answered was usually the important one. On the library project we never found out whether people got the right book; on benefits enrolment we never found out whether people chose better plans. Both would have been expensive to measure and both were the point.

Do you do visual design?

Competently, not excellently, and the matrix says so in two places. On a project where the interface has to be beautiful I would rather work alongside somebody who is genuinely good at it, and I have twice, and both times the work was better.

What if we cannot give you access to users?

Then we do the work anyway and everything I produce is labelled as a hypothesis rather than as a finding, throughout, including in the final deck. It is a real constraint and it is not a dealbreaker. What is a dealbreaker is being told there is access and then finding out in week four that there is not.
