Why that happens

You have been looking at the wrong version of your face your whole life.

The mirror is reversed and you are used to it

Every mirror you have ever used shows you a flipped face. Faces are not symmetrical, so the flipped one is genuinely a different arrangement — and it is the one you know. A photograph shows the version everyone else has always seen, and it looks subtly wrong to you and completely normal to them.

Familiarity is doing the liking, not beauty

People reliably prefer the version of a face they have seen most often. Your friends prefer your photographs. You prefer your mirror. Neither of you is wrong, and only one of you is looking at something other people can see.

A face at rest is not a face being unfriendly

A relaxed face has a downturned mouth and heavy lids, and reads as displeased in a still frame in a way it never does in motion. Most people who say they look angry in photographs are looking at their own face at rest for the first time.

Lenses change the geometry, and close ones are unkind

A phone camera held at arm’s length enlarges whatever is nearest it, which is your nose. Stepping back and using a longer lens flattens that out. Most self-perception about noses is a lens problem, and it is ours to solve, not yours.

None of this is reassurance. It is the reason a sitting takes an hour instead of ten minutes: we are waiting for the point where you stop watching yourself.

Retouching

We remove what is temporary. We keep what is you.

This is a line, not a slider, and it is the same line for everybody. If you want it moved we will move it and say so on the invoice, but we will tell you first what it costs the picture.

What comes out

  • A spot that will be gone in a fortnight
  • A stray hair across the face
  • Dust, sensor marks, a shine from the lamp
  • A bra strap, a label, a crooked collar
  • Something distracting in the background

What stays

  • Lines, and where they came from
  • Scars, birthmarks, freckles, moles
  • The shape of your nose and jaw
  • Your teeth, as they are
  • Your body, at the size it is

The reason is practical rather than moral. A retouched face stops looking like the person, and the people who love you notice immediately even when they cannot say what changed.

The hour

Nothing usable happens in the first twenty minutes.

One sitting a day, because the last twenty minutes are the ones worth having.

What happens during a one-hour sitting, in order
WhenWhatWhy
0–10 min Tea, and no camera in the room Nothing is being photographed. This is not a warm-up exercise, it is genuinely just talking.
10–25 min The camera comes out, on a tripod On a tripod so there is a face behind it rather than a lens. Most of these frames are of someone being photographed.
25–40 min You see the back of the camera Always. Seeing three good frames early is the single fastest way to stop bracing.
40–60 min The part we are here for Somewhere in here you forget. Every frame we have ever delivered comes from this stretch.

About thirty finished frames within ten days, and a call to go through them if you want one. You choose; we do not pick for you.

Common questions

Before you book.

What if I freeze up?

You will, for about the first half hour. Everyone does and the hour is built around it. The first frames are of someone being photographed; the ones we deliver come from after you stop noticing.

Can you make me look thinner?

No, and it is the one request we always decline. It never looks like the person afterwards, and the people who love you spot it instantly even when they cannot name what changed. We can light you well, which is a real answer to the same question.

Do I need professional hair and make-up?

No. Some people like it and it photographs fine, but a sitting where you do not recognise yourself in the mirror before we start is a harder sitting, not an easier one.

What should I wear?

Something you have worn before and were comfortable in. New clothes photograph as new clothes. Avoid a print so loud it becomes the subject, and bring a second option in case the first fights the light.

Do you use flash?

Almost never. The studio faces north and the window is the light. It means we shoot in daylight hours and it means the pictures look like the room you were actually sitting in.

Can I see everything you shot?

You can see the whole take if you ask, and about one person a year does. Mostly it is two hundred frames of you blinking, and looking at them makes people feel worse rather than better.
