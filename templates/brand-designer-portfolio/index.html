The rules are for the people who aren’t me.

An identity is not judged on the day it is delivered. It is judged eighteen months later, on a slide made by a receptionist at four in the afternoon and a sign ordered by a franchisee from a local fabricator. Below is one identity, ten rules, and how each one actually held — including the two I got wrong.

The eighteen-month audit See the identity

Kestrel Ferry Line · Delivered March 2025 · audited September 2026

10 rules, applied 2,566 times. 2 of them should not exist.

Eighteen months, one operator, eleven landings and six vessels. The two rules marked Drop are the useful part of this page: rule 9 was never broken because nobody ever wanted to break it, which makes it filler in a document that is already too long, and rule 10 was taste with a rule number on it. A guideline that contains rules people are right to ignore teaches everyone that the rules are optional.

Rule 1

One ink for type

“All text is Ink. Not Flag, not a tint of Ink, not black.”

401 held of 412 applied Keep

Eleven times, all in one franchise office using a Word template from 2019 that nobody could edit. Fixed by sending a new template rather than by writing a longer rule.

Rule 2

The mark sits on Sand or on white

“Never on a photograph. If the only option is a photograph, use the wordmark on a Sand bar.”

176 held of 188 applied Keep

Twelve times on social, always over a sunset. The escape hatch in the second sentence is why it is only twelve.

Rule 3

Half the mark’s height of clear space

“Nothing within half the height of the mark on any side. Measure with the mark itself.”

305 held of 340 applied Keep

Thirty-five times, mostly on 1:1 social avatars where half a height does not fit in the crop. That is a real gap and the fix is a separate avatar lockup, which now exists.

Rule 4

Never smaller than 22 mm

“Below 22 mm wide, use the K mark alone. On screen that is 96 px.”

61 held of 96 applied Rewrite

Thirty-five times on ticket stock, where the printable area is 19 mm and always was. The rule was written from a letterhead and never checked against a ticket. Rewritten to 18 mm with a thicker mark.

Rule 5

Sentence case everywhere

“Headings, buttons, signs, ticket stubs. No all-caps except on the hull.”

511 held of 520 applied Keep

Nine times on wayfinding, where a fabricator defaulted to caps. Cheap to fix and it is the rule people follow most easily, because it is the one they can check without a ruler.

Rule 6

One typeface, two weights

“Regular and Bold. If it is not installed, use Georgia and do not apologise.”

598 held of 640 applied Keep

Forty-two times, all of them a substitute font on a machine without the licence. The second sentence is the entire reason this rule works, and it took three identities to learn to write it.

Rule 7

Flag never carries type

“Flag is a fill. Text on it fails contrast at 3.73:1 and it is not a judgement call.”

204 held of 210 applied Keep

Six times, twice by me. The number in the rule is what makes it enforceable — a rule that says “avoid” gets argued with and a rule that says 3.73:1 does not.

Rule 8

Photography is people working

“Crew, ropes, weather, freight. No empty decks and no sunsets without a person in them.”

41 held of 74 applied Rewrite

Thirty-three times, because the marketing lead genuinely needed a sunset for the summer campaign and the rule gave nowhere to go. Rewritten with a named exception rather than pretending the pressure does not exist.

Rule 9

The mark is never rotated or outlined

“Not on merchandise, not for an anniversary, not once.”

31 held of 31 applied Drop

Never broken, and it never would have been. It is a rule against something nobody wanted to do, which means it is decoration in a document people already find too long. Dropped so the other nine get read.

Rule 10

No gradients anywhere

“Flat colour only, in every medium.”

38 held of 55 applied Drop

Seventeen times, always on screen, always where a gradient was doing something a flat fill could not — a long deck panel, a fade behind a header photograph. I wrote it out of taste rather than out of a constraint and it cost the identity credibility every time somebody sensibly ignored it.

Across the whole system, 2,366 of 2,566 applications held. The audit rewrote 2 rules and deleted 2, which is the only outcome that makes the fortnight worth paying for.

Rules 3 and 4, drawn

A rule with a number in it survives an argument.

A fabricator can check both of these with a ruler and without phoning anybody, which is the whole test. Both drawings are generated from the figures in the rules above.

7 touchpoints · 1 of them has opened the document

Who actually applies this.

Two of seven have opened the guidelines. That is not a failure of the client and it is not unusual — it is the normal condition of every identity that has ever been used by people with other jobs. The document is for the two. The files, the templates and the ten short rules are for the five.

Five tests, applied to every sentence

How a rule gets written.

  1. It says what to do, not what to avoid“All text is Ink” is followable. “Avoid using Flag for text” requires the reader to already know what to use instead, at four in the afternoon, in Word.
  2. It contains a number wherever possibleHalf a mark height. 22 mm. 3.73:1. A rule with a number in it is checkable by somebody who is not a designer, and it survives an argument with a fabricator.
  3. It names the escape hatchWhat to do when the ideal is impossible — a substitute typeface, a Sand bar over a photograph. Rules without escape hatches are broken silently; rules with them are broken visibly and correctly.
  4. It is testable in one glanceIf checking compliance needs a ruler and a colour picker, only a designer will ever check it, which means it will be checked twice.
  5. It earns its placeEvery rule costs attention from the ones around it. Ten rules that get read beat forty that get skimmed, and this audit exists to delete rules rather than to add them.

The sign comes before the logo.

Six stages, in the order that produces a mark which survives, rather than in the order a pitch deck presents them.

  1. The sign specFirst, before the mark is finished. A sign is one colour, 8 mm of stroke at 40 metres, and it constrains the mark more than any other application. Designing the sign last is how a beautiful mark becomes an unreadable one at a ferry landing.
  2. The one-colour versionSecond. Stamped parts, embroidery, a fax header that still exists. Anything that survives one colour survives everything.
  3. The small versionThird. 22 mm, then 18 mm, then the avatar crop. Scale down before scaling up; the large version is never the problem.
  4. The type systemFourth, and chosen for what is installable rather than for what is beautiful. The second-best typeface that everybody has beats the best one that three people have licensed.
  5. The colourFifth. Last, deliberately. Colour is the part everybody has an opinion about and the part that survives least intact through print, vinyl, fabric and screen.
  6. The documentLast, and short. Ten rules, four pages, and a folder of files people can use without reading anything.

Four things it is not

Not the logo, and not the document.

Not the logo
The mark is the smallest deliverable and the one everybody talks about. It appears on this system in seven sizes and one colour, and it took about a tenth of the four months.
Not the guidelines document
The document records the decisions. Five of the seven people applying this identity have never opened it and produced correct work anyway, because the files and the templates carried the rules instead.
Not a mood board of adjectives
“Confident, human, nautical” describes eleven thousand companies. A rule that says all text is Ink describes exactly one, and it can be followed.
Not finished at delivery
This audit is the deliverable that matters. It cost a fortnight, it deleted two rules and rewrote two more, and it is the only reason I know rule 4 was written from a letterhead.

Three things that actually get used.

A folder of the right file, per use
One-colour EPS, RGB SVG, a 1:1 avatar lockup, and a 48 px favicon that somebody has actually looked at. Named for where they go, not for what they are.
Templates, not instructions
A Word letterhead, a slide deck, a notice template with the type already set. This is what the office actually uses and it is the highest-leverage hour in any identity project.
The ten rules on one page
A single side, no images, printable, pinnable. The four-page document exists too and is read by the two people who would have got it right regardless.

What I need from you.

The worst application you have
The ugliest sign, the oldest van, the form letter from 2019. It tells me more than a brief does, and it is where the constraints live.
Whoever actually makes things
The office manager who builds the notices, half an hour, once. Every identity I have got right had that conversation and every one I have got wrong did not.
A real measurement of the smallest thing
The printable area on the ticket. The stitch count. The 8 mm stroke on the sign. One measurement, taken rather than assumed, is worth a month of revisions.
Permission to come back in eighteen months
A fortnight, a year and a half later, to find out which rules held. It costs less than any other part of the project and it is the only part that produces evidence.

Common questions

Mostly about the two I deleted.

Why publish rules you got wrong?

Because a guideline containing rules people are right to ignore teaches everybody that the rules are optional, and that costs the other eight. Rule 10 banned gradients out of taste rather than out of a constraint, and every sensible person who ignored it learned something about how much the document was worth.

Is an audit really a separate piece of work?

A fortnight, eighteen months after delivery. It is the cheapest thing on my rate sheet and the only part of any identity project that produces evidence rather than opinion. Two of these ten rules would still be in the document without it.

What if we cannot install the typeface everywhere?

Then it is the wrong typeface, and that is a decision to make in month one rather than a problem to discover in month twenty. Rule 6 names Georgia as the fallback in the rule itself, which is why forty-two substitutions did not damage the system.

How long should a guidelines document be?

Short enough to be read by somebody who does not want to read it. Ten rules on one printable side, plus a folder of files named for where they go. The long document is for the two people who would have got it right anyway.

Do you design the logo first?

No. The sign specification comes first, before the mark is finished, because a sign is one colour at forty metres and constrains the mark more than any other application. Colour comes last, because it is the part everyone has an opinion about and the part that survives least intact through vinyl, fabric and screen.

Why is your own site almost colourless?

Because the only saturated colour on it belongs to the client. A brand designer’s portfolio competing with the work inside it is a common failure and a slightly embarrassing one. The one place colour appears here is inside the specimen blocks, which is where it belongs.
