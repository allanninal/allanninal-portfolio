A Quarter Of The World Has No Safe-Water Number At All

The WHO/UNICEF ladder has two rungs that get quoted as one. “At least basic” is a clean source within a half-hour walk. “Safely managed” is that source on the premises, working when you turn it on, and free of contamination — and it is the SDG 6.1.1 indicator. For 62 countries and 2.21 billion people, the second number does not exist.

Key Takeaways

62 of the 209 countries that publish a basic drinking water figure publish no safely-managed figure at all — 2.21 billion people, 27.31% of the population those estimates cover. Safely managed is the SDG 6.1.1 indicator, so for a quarter of the world the goal is being measured with a number nobody has.

  • The largest is China, 1,409 million people, published at 96.12% basic and blank on safely managed. 16 of the 62 are high-income countries — Australia and Saudi Arabia among them — so this is not a story about survey capacity.
  • Where both rungs exist they are far apart: a median 18.4 points across 147 countries, and 38 of them 40 points or more. Nepal is 93.64% basic and 16.49% safely managed.
  • Indonesia reports 89.01% basic and 30.46% safely managed across 283 million people. The Philippines reports 95.86% and 48.48% — a 47.38-point difference between the two ways of saying the same sentence.
  • The world went from 61.26% safely managed in 2000 to 73.74% in 2024: 0.52 points a year. Held at that rate, universal coverage arrives in 2075 — 45 years after the 2030 target.
  • Progress is not uniform. 30 of the 145 countries with readings in both years were lower in 2022 than in 2015, the year the goals were adopted. Rural coverage worldwide is 55.87% against 83.51% urban.

The Indicator That Is Not There

Every country in this dataset is measured against SDG target 6.1, and the indicator for it is the safely-managed share. For 62 of them that share has never been estimated. They are not zeroes and they are not low numbers — the column is blank.

2030, Arriving In 2075

Two Publishers, One Set Of Estimates

These figures come from the WHO/UNICEF Joint Monitoring Programme. Two organisations republish them and neither carries the whole series, so both are read and every overlapping value is compared.

The cross-check

Every country-year-indicator pair the two publishers share is compared and the fetch aborts if any disagrees. 19,177 pairs overlap and the worst disagreement is 0.0 points. That verifies the copies match each other, not that the underlying estimates are right.

Neither one is complete

970 pairs appear only in WHO's copy and 4,003 only in the World Bank's. Reading either alone would have quietly dropped part of the record, and the union is what this page uses.

A null that is not a zero

WHO publishes open defecation at country level with a null in the numeric field and a whole-percent string beside it. Reading it the way every other indicator here is read drops 14,023 rows while the request still returns 200. It is taken from the World Bank instead and checked against WHO's rounded copy, where the worst gap across 4,993 pairs is exactly half a point — which is what rounding to a whole number allows and no more.

What These Numbers Are Not

A share of a population is a modelled estimate built from household surveys and censuses, not a meter reading. Four limits matter for everything above.

A blank is not a zero

The 62 countries with no safely-managed figure are excluded from every average on this page rather than counted as zero. That is the honest treatment and it also means every world figure here describes the countries that report, not the world.

Estimates, not measurements

JMP fits a regression through survey points, so a country's annual series is smoother than reality and a single year's change is rarely a single year's event. This is why the page reads 2015 against 2022 rather than year on year.

Handwashing is the thinnest series

Only 91 of the 214 countries have a basic-handwashing figure and 123 do not, so no world map is drawn from it here. It is in the CSVs for anyone who wants it, with the gaps visible.

National averages hide their own tails

The urban and rural split above is one cut of that, and it is the only one these estimates support. A national figure of 73.74% says nothing about which households inside the country are in the other 26.26%.

Key Findings & Summary

  • 62 of 209 countries publish a basic drinking water figure and no safely-managed one: 2.21 billion people, 27.31% of the population covered. That missing figure is the SDG 6.1.1 indicator.
  • 16 of them are high-income countries, so the gap is not explained by survey capacity alone.
  • Where both rungs exist the median distance is 18.4 points across 147 countries, and 38 are forty points or more apart.
  • The world moved 61.26% to 73.74% between 2000 and 2024, 0.52 points a year. Universal coverage at that rate arrives in 2075, 45 years late.
  • 30 of 145 countries were lower in 2022 than in 2015. Rural coverage worldwide is 55.87% against 83.51% urban.
  • Open defecation fell from 21.05% to 4.34% of the world. The bottom of the ladder moved a great deal; the top did not.
