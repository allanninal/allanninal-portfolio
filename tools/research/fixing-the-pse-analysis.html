01 — The verdict

Rebuild it. Do not patch it.

The question you asked

Whether the Yahoo Finance citation should be dropped, or the 2014–2024 series rebuilt from PSE Edge.

The answer is neither, because the premise was too small. The citation is not stale — it is impossible. Neither named source could have produced this series. And the series itself is mostly right, but the page around it carries errors far more serious than a bad footnote, including six sector returns that are wrong, two of them with the sign flipped.

The good news is that the underlying work was real. Nine of the eleven year-end closes are correct to the peso, every year-over-year figure is correctly derived from its own inputs, and the implied 2013 close sits within a few points of the actual one. Somebody did pull real data.

The bad news is that the two wrong closes poison the rows beneath them, the sector chart contradicts the index chart on the same page, the top-ten table omits the largest company on the exchange, and the page states three different values for the PSEi all-time high — none of which is the real one.

Rebuilding is also, at this point, the cheaper option. The complete daily history from 1987 to today is one HTTP request, no key, no scraping. Patching individual numbers would take longer and still leave the page internally contradictory.

2021 flips the sign of the year. The page tells readers 2021 was a recovery on the back of the vaccine rollout, up 2.8%. The PSEi actually finished 2021 slightly down, at 7,122.63. The figure 7,338 is not a year-end value at all — the index passed through the 7,300s in November before giving it back.

2024 is a visible placeholder. The close is the 2023 value copied down, with a "0.0% flat consolidation" note attached. The real 2024 close was 6,528.79, up 1.22%. Two identical adjacent rows in a table of market closes is the kind of thing a reader notices before they read a word of the analysis.

The 2022 row is collateral damage. Its close is correct, but its −10.5% is computed against the invented 2021 base. The real 2022 return was −7.81%. One bad cell propagated into a second wrong number.

The same placeholder also corrupts the page's headline decade figure. "PSEi returned −10.8% over the decade 2014–2024" is arithmetically the 2014 to 2023 return. The real 2014→2024 return is −9.71%.

03 — Three answers to one question

The all-time high, stated three ways

This is the most damaging error, because it is the single most-Googled fact about the PSEi and it sits on Wikipedia's infobox.

What the paired pages claim the record is
WhereClaimVerdict
Project page — key takeaways8,558 in 2017wrong by ~500
Project page — callout box7,230 in 2014wrong by ~1,830
Project page — 2014 table row"All-time high"wrong
Blog postnear 9,000 in early 2018essentially right

The actual record

9,058.62 closing, 9,078.37 intraday — both on Monday 29 January 2018. Confirmed as the maximum close across all 9,932 sessions from January 1987 to today, and corroborated independently by Wikipedia and TradingEconomics.

8,558.42 was a record close for about one trading month: set 29 December 2017, beaten on 26 January 2018, beaten again three days later. Presenting it as the all-time high in 2026 is wrong. Presenting 7,230 as the all-time high is wrong by a wider margin — it was not even the record at the time, since the index closed at 8,127.48 in April 2015.

Worth noting for the repair: the blog post has been right all along. "Peaked near 9,000 in early 2018" was in the original text and survived the plain-language rewrite. So the pair has contradicted itself since publication, and the correct answer is already sitting on one half of it.

Only the shape survives: Services really did lead and Mining really did lag. Every magnitude is wrong, and Property and Holding Firms are shown gaining when both fell — Property by nearly 17%.

There is also an internal tell that did not need any external data to catch. Weighting the page's own claimed sector returns by the page's own claimed sector weights implies a market up about 4.4% in 2024. The same page's index table says 2024 returned 0.0%. The two charts cannot both be true.

The narrative attached to the chart is wrong too. It credits the Services sector's performance to "BPO revenue growth surpassing $35 billion." There is no material listed BPO on the PSE — the industry's giants are listed abroad. The Services index is driven by ICTSI's ports business, plus PLDT and Globe. The $35B figure is also a 2023 vintage; the industry now headlines $40B.

06 — Why the sources are impossible

Neither cited source could have produced this

The page credits "PHISIX API & Yahoo Finance (.PS stocks)". Both were investigated directly.

PHISIX never carried the index

PHISIX is a community app that scraped the PSE site and re-served it as JSON. Two findings kill it as the source of an index series. First, it has never served the PSEi at all — its full 388-record payload contains only equities, and the only PSE-ish symbol in it is PSE, the listed exchange operator, not the index. Second, even for individual stocks its history does not reach back past about 2016, and it offers only one-symbol-one-date lookups with no range endpoint.

It is also on borrowed time: the main host was taken down after a legal notice in December 2023. Two mirrors still answer, which is not a foundation to build on.

Yahoo's individual PH stocks froze in 2019

Tickers like SMPH.PS return exchange YHD, a null price, instrument type MUTUALFUND, and a last-updated stamp of 28 June 2019.

So whatever produced the nine correct closes, it was not the two sources named on the page. That has to be corrected regardless of what else is fixed — a citation that could not have produced the data is worse than no citation, because it invites a reader to check and find nothing.

07 — A correction

I over-generalised about Yahoo

Correcting my earlier finding

Earlier in this work I reported that "Yahoo Finance no longer carries Philippine equities" and treated the whole feed as dead. That was true of individual stocks, but I extended it too far.

The index is alive and current. PSEI.PS returns exchange "Philippine", instrument type INDEX, a live price, and a complete daily history: 9,932 sessions from 2 January 1987 to today. It matches the PSE's own feed to the centavo on every overlapping year.

This changes the recommendation for the better — the backfill problem I described earlier largely disappears — so it is worth stating plainly rather than quietly folding in.

08 — The recommended fix

Seven steps, one afternoon

  1. Pull the real series and check it in

    One request to PSEI.PS gives every daily close since 1987. Derive year-end closes, write them to data/ph-pse/psei_annual.csv with a source column, exactly as the dengue and tourism projects already do. That checked-in file becomes the system of record.

  2. Cross-check against the exchange, and say so

    Fetch frames.pse.com.ph/compositeSector and confirm the overlapping years match. They do today, to the centavo, for 2021–2025. A methodology note saying two independent sources agree is worth more than either citation alone.

  3. Fix 2021 and 2024, and everything downstream

    Correct the two closes, then recompute the 2022 and 2024 year-over-year figures and the decade return (−9.71%, not −10.8%). Extend the series through 2025 while you are there — the page is already a year stale.

  4. Replace all six sector returns

    The same compositeSector response carries all six sector indices. Two of the published numbers point the wrong way, so this is a correction of fact, not of precision.

  5. Settle the all-time high at 9,058.62 on 29 January 2018

    Fix it in all three places on the project page. The blog post already says it correctly — align the project to the blog, not the other way round.

  6. Re-pull or retire the top-10 table

    It omits the largest company on the exchange and mixes five vintages, so no as-of stamp can rescue it. Either rebuild it from one dated snapshot — ICTSI first — or drop it and keep the sector view, which the data supports.

  7. Replace the source line and add an as-of date

    Drop PHISIX and Yahoo-.PS-stocks. Cite the index feed and the PSE cross-check. Then stamp the market-cap, turnover and listed-company figures with the date they were true, or correct them: ≈₱13.65T for 2025, ≈₱3.9B daily turnover, 283 listed.

One design note that matters

The PSE's own feed is a rolling five-year window. If you ever switch the pipeline to re-derive the whole series from it on each run, the early years will silently disappear. Keep the checked-in CSV authoritative and append to it; treat the live feeds as verification, not as storage.

10 — What holds up

Worth keeping

An audit that only lists faults gives a false picture. These parts of the page are sound and should survive the rebuild:

  • Nine of eleven year-end closes, exact to the peso.
  • Every year-over-year calculation, correctly derived from its own inputs — the arithmetic was never the problem.
  • "Sharpest single-day decline in PSE history" for 19 March 2020, verified against 39 years of daily data at −13.34%.
  • The −33.6% crash window, correct for 27 February to 19 March 2020.
  • The listed-company count, 282 against a real 283.
  • The ASEAN-underperformance ranking, though the margin deserves softening.
  • The "key event" column — Duterte 2016, Build Build Build 2017, TRAIN-era inflation 2018, BSP cuts 2019, COVID 2020, rate hikes 2022 — all defensible, apart from the 2014 "all-time high" label.
  • The blog post's account of the peak, which was right before this audit started.

The page was built on real data by someone who did the arithmetic correctly. What it lacks is a verification pass and a source line that matches how the numbers were actually obtained. Both are one afternoon's work, and the result is a page that a sceptical reader can check.

Method. Two research sweeps plus direct verification. The index series was pulled locally and recomputed rather than taken on trust: 9,932 daily sessions, 1987-01-02 to 2026-09-04. Year-end closes and 2024 sector returns were confirmed independently against the PSE's own compositeSector feed. Single-day declines and crash windows were computed across the full series.

Unverified, and flagged as such. The 4,039 intraday print for 19 March 2020 could not be confirmed from any series pulled. The average-daily-turnover figure could not be checked against a full-year PSE statistic; the comparison shown is to a single recent session. Market-cap and top-10 figures come from a third-party aggregator, not the exchange.
